## About the Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) Calculator

**Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)** is a crucial metric used by investors and businesses to measure the mean annual growth rate of an investment over a specified period of time longer than one year. It represents one of the most accurate ways to calculate and determine returns for anything that can rise or fall in value over time.

---

## The Formula

The formula for CAGR is:

$$ \text{CAGR} = \left( \frac{\text{Ending Value}}{\text{Beginning Value}} \right)^{\frac{1}{n}} - 1 $$

Where:
- **Ending Value** = The final value of the investment
- **Beginning Value** = The initial value of the investment
- **$n$** = Number of years

**Example:**
You invest $10,000 in a portfolio. After 5 years, it is worth $16,000.
$$ \text{CAGR} = \left( \frac{16000}{10000} \right)^{\frac{1}{5}} - 1 = (1.6)^{0.2} - 1 = 1.0985 - 1 = \mathbf{9.85\%} $$

This means that if your investment had grown at exactly 9.85% every single year, it would have reached $16,000. 

---

## Why Use CAGR Instead of Average Return?

Average return can be highly deceptive due to volatility. 
Imagine an investment of $100:
- Year 1: +100% (Value is now $200)
- Year 2: -50% (Value is back to $100)

The *average* return is $(100\% - 50\%) / 2 = 25\%$. You might think you made money. But in reality, your $100 is still exactly $100. The total return is 0%.
The **CAGR** calculation correctly outputs **0%**, because it only looks at the start and end values over the time horizon.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does CAGR account for risk?**
No. CAGR assumes a smooth, steady growth rate every year. Two investments could both have a 10% CAGR over 10 years, but one might have been extremely volatile (jumping up and down 40% a year) while the other was a steady 10% bond. Risk metrics like standard deviation or the Sharpe ratio must be used alongside CAGR.

**Can I use CAGR for time periods less than a year?**
While mathematically possible (by using fractions of a year for $n$), it is highly discouraged. Annualizing short-term returns (e.g., taking a 10% gain over 1 month and projecting a 213% annualized return) is almost always misleading and dangerous.
