## The Formula

Calculating a discount involves simple percentage math.

$$ \text{Amount Saved} = \text{Original Price} \times \left(\frac{\text{Discount Percentage}}{100}\right) $$

$$ \text{Final Price} = \text{Original Price} - \text{Amount Saved} $$

**Example:** A TV originally costs $800, and it is on sale for 25% off.
- Amount Saved = $800 \times 0.25 = \$200
- Final Price = $800 - \$200 = **\$600**

## Double Discounts (Stacked Discounts)

Retailers often run "take an additional X% off the clearance price" sales. **You cannot just add the percentages together.** A 20% discount plus an additional 10% discount is NOT a 30% total discount. The second discount is applied to the already-reduced price.

**Example:** A $100 shirt is on clearance for 20% off. You have a coupon for an extra 10% off.
- Step 1: $100 \times 0.20 = \$20 saved. New price is $80.
- Step 2: $80 \times 0.10 = \$8 saved. Final price is **\$72**.

The total discount is $28, which is a **28% discount**, not 30%.

## Fixed Amount Discounts vs. Percentage

Sometimes you have a choice between a fixed dollar coupon (e.g., "$15 off your purchase") and a percentage coupon (e.g., "20% off"). Which is better?

It depends entirely on the total price. The **"Rule of 100"** is a common retail psychology principle:
- If the item costs **less than $100**, a percentage discount (like 25% off a $20 shirt) sounds like a better deal than the dollar equivalent ($5 off).
- If the item costs **more than $100**, a fixed dollar discount (like $50 off a $500 laptop) sounds like a better deal than the percentage equivalent (10% off).

Use our calculator to test both scenarios and ensure you always use the coupon that saves you the most absolute dollars.
