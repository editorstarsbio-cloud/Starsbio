## About the Ideal Gas Law Calculator

The **Ideal Gas Law** is the master equation of thermodynamics for gas behavior. It unifies three separate historical gas laws (Boyle's Law, Charles's Law, and Avogadro's Law) into one comprehensive relationship between pressure, volume, temperature, and amount of gas.

---

## The Formula

$$ PV = nRT $$

Where:
- **$P$** = Absolute pressure (in Pascals, Pa, or atmospheres, atm)
- **$V$** = Volume (in liters or m³)
- **$n$** = Number of moles of gas
- **$R$** = Universal gas constant = 8.314 J/(mol·K) = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K)
- **$T$** = Absolute temperature (must be in **Kelvin**, not Celsius or Fahrenheit)

**Temperature conversion:** $T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15$

**Example:** How many moles of oxygen gas are in a 10 L container at 2 atm and 25°C?
- $P = 2$ atm, $V = 10$ L, $T = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15$ K

$$ n = \frac{PV}{RT} = \frac{2 \times 10}{0.08206 \times 298.15} = \frac{20}{24.47} \approx 0.817 \text{ mol} $$

## The Three Derived Gas Laws

| Law | Relationship | Constant |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Boyle's Law | $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$ | Temperature, moles |
| Charles's Law | $V_1/T_1 = V_2/T_2$ | Pressure, moles |
| Gay-Lussac's | $P_1/T_1 = P_2/T_2$ | Volume, moles |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When does the Ideal Gas Law fail?**
The ideal gas law assumes gas molecules have no volume and no intermolecular attractions. It breaks down at very high pressures (molecules become closely packed) or very low temperatures (intermolecular forces become significant). For these conditions, the **Van der Waals equation** is used.

**Why must temperature be in Kelvin?**
The gas law equations require an absolute temperature scale. Celsius has an arbitrary zero point (the freezing point of water). Kelvin starts at absolute zero (−273.15°C), the point of zero molecular motion. Using Celsius would produce mathematically incorrect results.
