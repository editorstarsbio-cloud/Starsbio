## How Marginal Tax Brackets Work

The most common misconception about taxes is that moving into a higher tax bracket means *all* your income is taxed at that higher rate. **This is completely false.** The US uses a progressive, marginal tax system. 

If you get a raise that pushes you into the 22% bracket, only the dollars you earn *above* that bracket line are taxed at 22%. The rest of your money is still taxed at 10% and 12%.

**Example (Simplified):**
Imagine a tax system with two brackets:
- 10% on income from $0 to $50,000
- 20% on income over $50,000

If you earn $60,000:
- The first $50,000 is taxed at 10% ($5,000).
- The remaining $10,000 is taxed at 20% ($2,000).
- Total Tax = $7,000. 

Your "Marginal Rate" is 20% (the rate applied to your last dollar earned), but your "Effective Rate" (your total tax divided by total income) is only 11.6%.

## Standard Deduction vs. Itemized Deductions

Before the IRS applies the tax brackets to your income, you are allowed to subtract a certain amount of money from your total income. This is called a deduction.

1. **The Standard Deduction:** A fixed dollar amount that every taxpayer is allowed to take, no questions asked. For 2024, it is $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married couples. If you earn $60,000, you only pay taxes on $45,400.
2. **Itemized Deductions:** If you have massive medical bills, huge charitable donations, or pay a lot in mortgage interest, you can add all those up. If the total is *higher* than the Standard Deduction, you take this instead.

*Note: The vast majority of Americans (about 90%) take the Standard Deduction because the 2017 tax cuts doubled it.*

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a bonus get taxed higher than regular income?**
No, but it *feels* like it does. Employers usually withhold taxes on bonuses at a flat 22% rate (mandated by the IRS) because a bonus is supplemental income. However, when you file your taxes at the end of the year, that bonus is simply added to your total income. If your effective tax rate is only 15%, you will get that extra 7% withheld back as a tax refund.

**What is the difference between a deduction and a credit?**
A **deduction** lowers your *taxable income*. If you are in the 22% bracket, a $1,000 deduction saves you $220. A **credit** lowers your *actual tax bill* dollar-for-dollar. A $1,000 tax credit saves you exactly $1,000.
