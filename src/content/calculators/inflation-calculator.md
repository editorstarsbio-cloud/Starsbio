## About the Inflation Calculator

**Inflation** is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services rises, causing the purchasing power of currency to fall. Our **Inflation Calculator** helps you understand exactly how much money in the past is worth today, or how much today's money will be worth in the future.

---

## The Formula

The formula to calculate the future cost of an item due to inflation is a standard compound interest formula:

$$ \text{Future Value} = \text{Present Value} \times (1 + \text{Inflation Rate})^t $$

Where $t$ is the number of years.

**Example:** 
You want to know how much a $100 grocery bill will cost in 10 years if the inflation rate averages 3% per year.
$$ \text{Future Value} = 100 \times (1 + 0.03)^{10} = 100 \times 1.3439 = \mathbf{\$134.39} $$

This means that in 10 years, you will need $134.39 to buy the exact same amount of food that $100 buys today.

## The Rule of 72 and Inflation

You can use the "Rule of 72" to quickly estimate how long it will take for prices to double (or for your money's purchasing power to be cut in half).

$$ \text{Years to Double} = \frac{72}{\text{Inflation Rate}} $$

If inflation is 4%, prices will double in 18 years ($72 / 4 = 18$). If inflation spikes to 9%, prices will double in just 8 years.

## Why Does Inflation Happen?

Economists generally divide inflation into three types:
1. **Demand-Pull Inflation:** Occurs when demand for goods outpaces the economy's ability to produce them. (Too many dollars chasing too few goods).
2. **Cost-Push Inflation:** Occurs when the cost of production increases (e.g., oil prices spike), forcing companies to raise prices to maintain profit margins.
3. **Built-In Inflation:** The psychological expectation of inflation. Workers demand higher wages to keep up with living costs, which causes businesses to raise prices to pay those wages, creating a wage-price spiral.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is inflation always a bad thing?**
No. Central banks (like the US Federal Reserve) typically target a small, steady inflation rate of around 2%. Mild inflation encourages people to spend and invest their money today (because it will be worth less tomorrow), which drives economic growth. Deflation (prices dropping) is often considered much worse, as it causes consumers to hoard money, stalling the economy.

**How is historical inflation measured?**
In the United States, it is most commonly measured by the CPI (Consumer Price Index), which tracks the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a market basket of consumer goods and services (food, housing, transportation, medical care).
