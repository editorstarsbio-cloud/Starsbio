## The Magic of Compound Interest

Compound interest is the process of earning interest on your principal, and then earning interest on that interest in the future. It is the fundamental mechanism that drives wealth creation in the stock market.

The human brain thinks linearly, but compound interest is exponential.
- If you save $500 a month under your mattress for 40 years, you will have **$240,000**.
- If you invest $500 a month in the S&P 500 at an 8% return for 40 years, you will have **$1.74 million**.

In the investing scenario, you still only contributed $240,000 out of your own pocket. The market generated the other $1.5 million for you automatically.

## Time in the Market > Timing the Market

The most important variable in the investment calculator is not the interest rate, nor the monthly contribution amount. It is **Time**. 

Because compounding is exponential, the final years of an investment journey generate mind-boggling returns. 

**Example:**
Person A invests $500 a month from age 25 to 35 (10 years) and then stops completely. (Total invested = $60,000).
Person B waits, and then invests $500 a month from age 35 to 65 (30 years). (Total invested = $180,000).

Assuming an 8% return, at age 65:
- Person B has **$745,000**.
- Person A has **$944,000**.

Person A invested a third as much money, but ends up with $200,000 more, simply because their money had 10 extra years to compound.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What return rate should I use?**
Historically, the US stock market (S&P 500) has returned about 10% annually before inflation, or about 7% to 8% after inflation. For long-term (10+ year) stock projections, 8% is a reasonable and conservative estimate. For bonds, 4-5% is typical. For cash in a high-yield savings account, 3-4% is currently standard.

**How often should I check my investments?**
If you are investing for retirement in diversified index funds, you should check your portfolio as rarely as possible—perhaps once or twice a year to rebalance. Checking a portfolio daily causes immense psychological stress and often leads to panic-selling during temporary market crashes, which destroys long-term returns.
